**The Alliance part improves supply and cost, but it is not yet a proven battery-energy improvement.** Its lower voltage reduces NAND power, while slower transfers can keep the MCU powered longer.

Compared with our original **MX30LF2G18AC-TI**, one packed RAW10 image occupies **486,220 bytes / 238 pages**:

| Operation | Original 3.3 V NAND | Alliance 1.8 V NAND |
|---|---:|---:|
| Transfer image and full spare areas | 10.1 ms | 23.3 ms |
| Internal programming, typical | 71.4 ms | 71.4 ms |
| **Combined write estimate** | **81.5 ms** | **94.7 ms** |
| Combined using maximum program times | 152.9 ms | 189.9 ms |

These calculations assume pre-erased storage, sequential programming, and each chip’s fastest permitted bus timing. They exclude firmware/ECC overhead and verification. Sources: [Macronix datasheet](https://www.macronix.com/Lists/Datasheet/Attachments/8461/MX30LF2G18AC%2C%203V%2C%202Gb%2C%20v1.4.pdf), [Alliance datasheet](https://www.alliancememory.com/wp-content/uploads/AllianceMemory_AS9Fxx_Series_1Gb_2Gb_4Gb_8Gb_x8bits_SLC_Parallel_NAND_Fl.pdf).

**That apparent 16% slowdown depends heavily on our controller configuration.** If both chips operate at a practical 50 ns bus cycle, the estimates become **96.5 versus 97.3 ms**—almost identical.

For power, the original NAND specifies **15 mA typical while programming**, or **49.5 mW at 3.3 V**. Alliance specifies **20 mA maximum**, or **36 mW at 1.8 V**, but does not publish typical programming current. At the typical programming duration, those figures yield **3.53 mJ versus 2.57 mJ**. This mixes typical and maximum current, so it is an illustrative comparison—not a measured saving.

The whole-board effect can reverse that benefit. If the MCU and other consumers draw **200 mW**, an additional **13.25 ms costs 2.65 mJ**. That exceeds the approximately **0.96 mJ programming-energy reduction** in the example above. NAND transfer energy and the broader savings from moving everything to 1.8 V still need accounting.

Two other costs matter:

- Full-image readback adds approximately **16 ms versus 30 ms**.
- Erasing four blocks adds **4 ms versus 14 ms typical**. Keeping erased blocks ready—or erasing on USB power—avoids this during a battery capture.

**My recommendation remains to qualify Alliance**, while prioritizing packed RAW10, prompt camera shutdown, and efficient MCU waits. Actual FMC speed and firmware behavior will determine whether battery energy improves.

Neither publishes enough operating-current and cold-start information to declare it the energy winner. ATP also has conflicting old/new family performance figures that need exact-revision confirmation. These industrial cards are considerably more expensive than the approximately $8 Alliance NAND. [Kingston specifications](https://www.kingston.com/datasheets/sdcit2_en.pdf)

**For our image size, transfer speed is already sufficient.** A packed photo occupies 486,400 bytes after sector padding. Using native four-bit SDMMC at 50 MHz gives a 25 MB/s raw bus ceiling; a reasonable **illustrative** payload rate of 20 MB/s transfers it in **24.3 ms**, compared with our approximately **94.7 ms NAND write model**.

However, microSD adds startup and commit delays. The SD specification permits initialization to take up to one second, and individual write-busy intervals can reach hundreds of milliseconds. Those are allowances, not typical card behavior—but they explain why sustained-speed ratings cannot predict one-photo performance. [SD Association specification](https://www.sdcard.org/cms/wp-content/themes/sdcard-org/dl.php?f=Part1_Physical_Layer_Simplified_Specification_Ver6.00.pdf)

We can initialize the card alongside camera startup, preserving the acquisition target. The following illustrates the resulting tradeoff:

| Storage case | Acquisition + save time | Storage-related battery energy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Alliance NAND partial reference | **145 ms** | **25 mJ** |
| SD ready in 20 ms; final commit adds 10 ms | **84 ms** | **19 mJ** |
| SD ready in 100 ms; final commit adds 25 ms | **149 ms** | **52 mJ** |

**These are scenarios, not predictions for Kingston or ATP.** They assume 50 ms acquisition, overlapping initialization, 50 mA card current during initialization/write, 200 mW for other loads, and 85% conversion efficiency. Energy excludes the common boot/capture cost; the NAND reference also excludes transfer, verification and erase energy. At an assumed **100 mA card current**, the two SD examples rise to approximately **29 and 81 mJ**.

That makes **startup and final commit more important than chasing maximum bandwidth**.

The hardware changes are manageable:

- Use **SDMMC2**, relocating the accelerometer interface and LED from its pins. The camera’s existing interface can remain.
- Budget a **switched 3.3 V card supply**, with the MCU’s SD I/O bank on the same voltage; the CPU and NOR can remain at 1.8 V.
- Sharing the camera’s 2.8 V source is potentially possible, but needs more voltage-margin and timing qualification. UHS 1.8 V signaling does **not** make the card itself a 1.8 V load. [ST hardware guidance](https://www.st.com/resource/en/application_note/an5967-getting-started-with-hardware-development-for-stm32n6-mcus-stmicroelectronics.pdf#page=28)

Peak current matters for our batteries: a **100 mA card load plus 200 mW elsewhere** would draw about **52 mA through each CR2032 at a 12 V stack**, assuming 85% efficiency. That needs a pulse-load check.

**My recommendation is to benchmark the Kingston first before changing the PCB.** Use packed RAW10, preallocated contiguous storage, DMA, and proper card-ready verification before shutdown. Measure cold-start energy and completion-time distributions—not just a continuous write test. microSD could win, but we currently have stronger evidence for feasibility than for a battery-life improvement.
