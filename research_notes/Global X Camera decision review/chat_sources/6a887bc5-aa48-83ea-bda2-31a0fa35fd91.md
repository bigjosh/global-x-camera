ASSISTANT: ## Bottom line

A **normal compact-camera xenon flash cannot be charged from zero in 50 ms from two or three ordinary AA cells**. The obstacle is instantaneous battery power, not total battery energy.

The practical choices are:

1. **Use a high-power LED for most or all of the 50 ms** — best reusable solution.
2. **Use a much smaller, roughly 1 J xenon flash** — technically possible from three high-rate NiMH cells, but substantially weaker.
3. **Keep a full-size xenon capacitor precharged while the unit is off** — immediate flash, but the capacitor will self-discharge and remains dangerously charged.
4. **Use a single-use photoflash bulb** — the only approach that very nearly satisfies all three requirements literally.

## Why full-power xenon cannot cold-start that fast

A representative compact-camera photoflash capacitor is approximately 100–220 µF charged to about 320 V. Analog Devices gives examples of 100 µF at 320 V charged from 3.6 V in 4.6 seconds, and 220 µF at 320 V charged from 5 V in 3.5 seconds. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

Stored energy is:

\[
E=\frac{1}{2}CV^2
\]

Assuming an optimistic 80% charger efficiency:

| Capacitor at 320 V | Stored energy | Required input power for 50 ms | Current from 3.6 V |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20 µF | 1.02 J | 25.6 W | 7.1 A |
| 100 µF | 5.12 J | 128 W | 35.6 A |
| 220 µF | 11.26 J | 282 W | 78.2 A |

Those input-current figures are optimistic because the AA pack voltage will sag badly under load.

Three series AA cells do **not** multiply current capability; the same current passes through every cell. Energizer rates its L91 lithium AA at 4 A pulse current and lists 120–300 mΩ typical internal resistance. Thus a three-cell series pack is still nominally a 4 A pulse source, approximately 18 W before voltage sag—not the 128–282 W needed. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

A high-rate NiMH AA is considerably better. Energizer lists approximately 30 mΩ internal resistance for a charged NiMH AA. Three such cells, with extremely low-resistance contacts and wiring, might plausibly supply roughly 8–10 A for 50 ms, although that is an engineering extrapolation rather than a guaranteed datasheet operating point. That puts the realistic input-power range around 25–35 W, enough for about **1–1.4 J** after converter loss—not 5–11 J. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

## Best reusable solution: a 20–50 ms LED pulse

If the camera can integrate light for 20–50 ms, do not store the energy first. Draw it directly from the batteries while the LED is illuminated.

A suitable architecture is:

```text
3 × high-rate NiMH AA
        │
mechanical switch or normally-open relay
        │
optional low-voltage, low-ESR input capacitor
        │
MOSFET + current-sense pulse limiter
        │
3 V high-power LED on copper-core PCB
        │
optic matched to the camera field of view
```

One current LED in the appropriate power class is the 3 V Cree XHP70.2. Cree lists a maximum of 9.6 A, 29 W and up to 4,124 lm. That is close to what three good NiMH AAs can supply for 50 ms when fully charged and connected through a very low-resistance holder. The particular 3 V XHP70.2 configuration is cool white and low CRI, however, so it is suitable for maximizing light but not ideal for accurate photographic color. Cree’s 3 V XHP35.2 family includes higher-CRI versions but at lower total output. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

An array of camera-flash LEDs is another route. As an illustrative example, the Lumileds LUXEON Flash 7 produces typically 260 lm at 1 A and about 3.2 V during a 20 ms pulse; it is rated for 1.5 A pulses as long as 200 ms. Ten devices at 1 A would therefore be approximately 2,600 lm while drawing about 32 W. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

Important limitations:

- This does **not** equal xenon’s instantaneous peak intensity. A 5 J xenon discharge occurring in roughly 1 ms represents kilowatts during the pulse; the LED is operating at tens of watts.
- It can nevertheless deliver useful integrated photographic exposure because the LED stays on 20–50 times longer.
- It will not stop motion as effectively as a 0.5–2 ms xenon pulse.
- Matching the illumination optic to the camera’s field of view is critical. A bare 120° LED wastes much of its output outside the image.
- A normal spring-loaded AA holder may introduce enough resistance to materially reduce output. The holder, contacts, wiring and switch should all be designed for approximately 10 A.
- Do not directly connect an uncontrolled LED to the cells. Use a fast analog current limiter or synchronous buck/buck-boost driver. The LED’s voltage/current curve is too steep for reliable open-loop operation.

A 10–50 mF low-voltage capacitor at the LED driver input can reduce the initial voltage dip and absorb wiring inductance, but it cannot supply the whole flash. For perspective, 10 mF charged to 4 V stores only 0.08 J.

### Battery chemistry tradeoff

- **Maximum flash power:** high-rate NiMH.
- **Maximum shelf life:** lithium/iron-disulfide AA such as L91, but limit LED input power to roughly the 15–18 W class.
- **Alkaline:** poor choice for this pulse-power application.

## A smaller xenon flash is possible

Three high-rate NiMH AAs could conceivably operate a custom 25–35 W flyback charger and charge approximately **20–25 µF to 320 V in 50 ms**:

\[
20\ \mu F,\ 320V \Rightarrow 1.02J
\]

That would require roughly 7–9 A from a 3.6 V pack, depending on converter efficiency. It would need:

- A custom high-current flyback transformer rather than a standard 0.5 A photoflash charger design.
- A low-voltage MOSFET capable of large repetitive peak currents.
- A photoflash-rated 320–350 V capacitor.
- A small xenon tube rated for approximately 1 J.
- A several-kilovolt trigger circuit.

It would have only about one-fifth the energy of a 100 µF/320 V flash. Because useful distance or guide number scales approximately with the square root of energy, its range would be about:

\[
\sqrt{\frac{1}{5}}\approx 0.45
\]

So it would provide roughly half the guide number of a 5 J flash with otherwise identical optics. It could still be useful for a close-range camera, but it is not equivalent to an ordinary compact-camera xenon flash.

## Precharging the xenon capacitor

A full-power xenon flash can fire essentially immediately if its capacitor was charged during the **previous** powered period:

```text
Previous power-on:
AA cells → slow charger → 100–220 µF at 320 V

Power-off:
mechanical battery disconnect
capacitor remains charged

Next power-on:
trigger xenon immediately
```

A photoflash charger can avoid a continuous high-voltage resistor divider; Analog Devices describes a topology in which the only remaining DC loads are capacitor self-leakage and rectifier-diode leakage. That allows the capacitor to retain most of its energy over some finite interval. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

But there is no genuinely leakage-free capacitor:

- A photoflash electrolytic will gradually lose voltage.
- A polypropylene film capacitor generally has lower leakage, but the required volume is enormous. As one scale example, a TDK 10 µF, 600 V polypropylene capacitor measures approximately 21 × 31 × 31.5 mm; roughly ten would be required to reach 100 µF. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}
- The diode, PCB surface contamination, trigger circuitry and voltage monitor can all add leakage.
- Retention time would have to be measured on the actual assembly at the maximum humidity and temperature.
- Leaving 5–11 J stored at 320 V in an apparently powered-off product creates a substantial service and shock hazard.

Thus precharging is reasonable when “off” lasts seconds or perhaps minutes and the actual decay is characterized. It is a poor design for an uncontrolled period of days or months.

## What actually gives zero off-current

A charger IC’s shutdown mode is not zero. For example, the LT3484 specifies up to 1 µA quiescent current in shutdown, plus separate leakage paths. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}

For practical zero battery current, use a **physical open contact**:

- Mechanical power switch.
- Normally-open relay energized by the external power-available signal.
- Mechanically latched contact.
- Removable battery contact or other galvanic disconnect.

A MOSFET load switch can get into the nanoampere range, but it is not literally zero. A physical contact also does not eliminate self-discharge inside a battery or capacitor; it only eliminates current through the external circuit.

## The literal solution: a flashbulb

A commercial single-use photoflash bulb stores the flash energy chemically. The AA battery supplies only the short ignition pulse, so there is:

- No capacitor charging delay.
- No quiescent circuit current while disconnected.
- Very high peak brightness.
- Excellent long-term stored-energy retention compared with an electrical capacitor.

Historical photoflash firing circuitry used a brief ignition current on the order of 1 A, easily obtainable from AA cells. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="8"}

The disadvantages are decisive for many applications: one bulb per photograph, heat, physical size, glass containment, replacement logistics and dwindling availability. But when only one flash is required, it is the cleanest match to the stated requirements.

## Recommended design

For a **reusable** unit, use **three high-rate NiMH AAs driving a 20–50 ms, approximately 25–30 W LED pulse**, with:

- A hard mechanical battery disconnect.
- A low-resistance battery interconnect.
- A 3 V high-power LED or parallel flash-LED array.
- Active pulse-current limiting.
- A reflector or TIR/Fresnel optic matching the camera’s field of view.
- Pulse length synchronized to the actual sensor exposure.

For a **sub-2 ms, motion-stopping flash**, the practical choices are a precharged xenon capacitor, an added high-rate secondary battery serving as a pulse reservoir, or a single-use flashbulb. A full 5–11 J xenon capacitor cannot be charged from ordinary AAs in 50 ms from a cold start.

A 320 V photoflash circuit also produces a multi-kilovolt trigger pulse and may carry hundreds of amperes in the xenon discharge path; the manufacturer explicitly limits such circuits to high-voltage-trained personnel. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"}

## Two necessary corrections

### 1. Do not intentionally reach core saturation

While the primary switch is on,

\[
I_P(t)\approx \frac{V_{\text{BAT}}t}{L_P}
\]

and the stored energy is

\[
E_L=\frac12 L_P I_P^2
\]

The switch should open at a controlled peak current **before** saturation. In a properly designed flyback transformer, most of the recoverable energy is stored in the air gap. Once the core saturates, magnetizing inductance collapses and primary current rises extremely rapidly; most subsequent input becomes winding, battery, and switch heating rather than useful additional magnetic energy. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

A current-sense comparator is preferable to detecting saturation. It also gives directly controlled flash energy because \(E=\frac12LI^2\).

### 2. Trigger voltage and main-discharge current require opposite transformer ratios

A photographic xenon tube has two very different electrical phases:

- Initial ionization generally requires approximately **2–20 kV** and roughly **1–100 mJ**.
- Once ionized, the main discharge has an effective internal impedance around **0.1–5 Ω** and eventually extinguishes when the tube voltage falls to roughly **10–100 V**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

A high-turns-ratio secondary is good for producing kilovolts, but it transforms the primary current downward. For example, with a 100:1 secondary ratio and 10 A in the primary, the initial secondary current is only about 0.1 A. That can create an ionization streamer, but it is unlikely to sustain the high-current plasma that produces an efficient photographic flash.

The workable version would therefore use **two secondary windings**:

```text
                                Very-high-turn trigger winding
                                ───────────────> trigger wire
                                          
3 AA cells ── primary ── MOSFET
                  ║
                  ║  Low-ratio, high-current power winding
                  ╚════════════> xenon anode/cathode
```

When the MOSFET opens:

1. The high-turn trigger winding develops several kilovolts on the tube’s external trigger electrode.
2. The low-turn power winding supplies the main discharge at much higher current.
3. The xenon arc clamps the transformer voltage as magnetic energy transfers into the tube.

That is substantially more credible than attempting to use one high-voltage secondary for both functions.

## The battery-energy calculation is still decisive

A representative Analog Devices digital-camera photoflash circuit uses a **130 µF, 330 V capacitor** and operates at approximately 320 V. That stores:

\[
E=\frac12(130\ \mu\text{F})(320\text{ V})^2
  \approx 6.7\text{ J}
\]

This is a representative scale rather than a universal value for every consumer flash. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

For one uninterrupted 50 ms primary-current ramp, neglecting all resistance:

\[
E=\frac12 V_{\text{BAT}}tI_{\text{PK}}
\]

Therefore:

\[
I_{\text{PK}}=\frac{2E}{V_{\text{BAT}}t}
\]

Using three NiMH cells at 3.6 V nominal:

| Stored energy | Required peak primary current | Required primary inductance |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.5 J | 5.6 A | 32 mH |
| 1.0 J | 11.1 A | 16 mH |
| 2.0 J | 22.2 A | 8.1 mH |
| 6.7 J | 74 A | 2.4 mH |

Those are ideal values. They exclude battery sag, winding resistance, switch loss, leakage inductance, core loss, and incomplete transfer into the tube.

A charged Energizer NiMH AA is specified at approximately 30 mΩ internal resistance. Three series cells therefore contribute about 90 mΩ before accounting for the holder and wiring. At 74 A, the cells alone would nominally drop about 6.7 V—more than the entire battery voltage. Using that resistance as an optimistic Thevenin model, the absolute maximum power transferable from a nominal 3.6 V pack is only approximately:

\[
P_{\max}=\frac{V^2}{4R}
=\frac{3.6^2}{4(0.09)}
\approx 36\text{ W}
\]

That is only 1.8 J during 50 ms, even at the theoretical maximum-power operating point and before every other loss. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

A realistic magnetic-energy output would consequently be more like:

- **0.2–0.5 J:** readily conceivable with good NiMH cells.
- **Approximately 1 J:** possible but demanding.
- **5–7 J:** not remotely practical from three AAs in 50 ms.

## The one-shot transformer would be unexpectedly large

There is another severe problem: the full 50 ms battery volt-seconds are applied in one uninterrupted pulse.

\[
Vt=3.6(0.050)=0.18\text{ V·s}
\]

For a ferrite core limited to about 0.3 T:

\[
NA_E \geq \frac{Vt}{\Delta B}
=\frac{0.18}{0.3}
=0.60\text{ m}^2\text{-turn}
\]

Even with a fairly large 4 cm² core cross-section, that requires approximately:

\[
N=\frac{0.60}{0.0004}\approx1500\text{ primary turns}
\]

carrying several amperes. That is not a compact flyback transformer. A laminated-steel or amorphous core operating at a higher flux density could reduce the turns substantially, but the device would still be much larger and heavier than a photoflash capacitor of equivalent energy.

A conventional high-frequency flyback charger avoids this because it resets the core every few microseconds. The transformer stores only millijoules per switching cycle and transfers thousands of small packets into the capacitor. The capacitor—not the transformer—holds the full joule-scale energy.

## The more practical xenon solution

For approximately a **1 J xenon flash**, use a conventional capacitor:

\[
C=\frac{2E}{V^2}
\]

At 320 V:

\[
C=\frac{2(1)}{320^2}
\approx 19.5\ \mu\text{F}
\]

So an approximately **18–22 µF, 330 V photoflash capacitor** would provide roughly 0.9–1.1 J.

Charging 1 J in 50 ms at 80% efficiency requires:

\[
P_{\text{IN}}=\frac{1}{0.050(0.8)}
=25\text{ W}
\]

That means approximately 7–9 A from three high-rate NiMH cells, depending on loaded battery voltage. This is severe but plausible with:

- Welded or exceptionally low-resistance battery contacts.
- A high-current MOSFET flyback charger.
- A transformer designed for approximately 25–30 W burst input.
- A current-limited startup.
- An ordinary xenon trigger transformer.

The capacitor would remain **completely empty while the system is off**, so capacitor leakage would be irrelevant. A physical battery disconnect would give zero circuit quiescent current. The high-voltage capacitor would exist only for approximately the final few milliseconds before firing.

## Assessment of your inductive proposal

Your proposed system can be made to work, particularly with the two-secondary arrangement. It is most attractive when:

- Eliminating the joule-scale capacitor is intrinsically valuable.
- A 0.1–0.5 J flash is sufficient.
- Transformer size is not critical.
- A 1–10 ms flash duration is acceptable.
- You can tolerate considerable custom magnetics development.

For a full consumer-camera-class flash, it does not solve the fundamental problem: **three AA cells cannot furnish approximately 6 J within 50 ms**. For a reduced approximately 1 J flash, the conventional high-frequency flyback plus a 20 µF photoflash capacitor will be smaller, easier to control, more efficient, and probably more reliable than storing the same joule magnetically.

## Zero quiescent-current implication

A **physical contact** can make the flash circuitry draw zero load current regardless of chemistry:

```text
battery pack
    │
mechanical switch or bistable relay
    │
200 W burst charger
    │
photoflash capacitor
```

There are two distinctions:

- All cells have intrinsic self-discharge, so the battery itself never has mathematically zero loss.
- Rechargeable lithium packs normally include a protection circuit or BMS that draws a small continuous current even when the external load is disconnected.

The simplest implementation of the strict requirement is therefore a **primary TLM pack plus mechanical disconnect**. For a rechargeable pack, zero current into the flash electronics is straightforward, but the protection electronics will usually remain connected to the cells.
