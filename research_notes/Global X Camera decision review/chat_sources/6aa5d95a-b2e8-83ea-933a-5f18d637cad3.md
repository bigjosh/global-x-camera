## 2026-09-12T22:59:36.663Z · 75902438-c132-4c43-9ee8-5f6b10b77c3d

USER: with typical raw nand flash chips, are the bad blocks static or do bad block develp over the lifetime of the unit?

ASSISTANT: **Both: raw NAND can ship with bad blocks, and additional bad blocks can develop during its operating life.** You cannot safely treat the initial bad-block map as permanent. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

### The two categories

**Factory bad blocks** are defects identified during manufacturing. The manufacturer marks them—typically using designated bytes in the page’s spare/out-of-band area—and your firmware must avoid them. A chip containing some factory bad blocks is not necessarily defective as a product; this is an anticipated part of NAND manufacturing. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

**Grown or runtime bad blocks** were initially usable but subsequently become unreliable. Repeated program/erase cycles stress the cells, eventually causing failures to program, erase, or reliably retain data. Electrical or other physical damage can also cause block failures. These require the firmware to retire additional blocks during use. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

### A bit error is not necessarily a bad block

NAND also requires **error-correcting codes, or ECC**, to handle bit errors in otherwise usable storage. An ECC-corrected error does **not**, by itself, mean the entire block should be discarded. ECC and bad-block management solve different problems: ECC recovers data within its correction capability; bad-block management avoids blocks that are no longer trustworthy. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

### What this means for your recorder

The firmware needs to do more than scan once:

- **Skip the factory-marked blocks** and persistently track newly retired ones. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}
- **Check the result of every program and erase operation.** Handle genuine failures by retiring the affected block and using another, following the selected chip’s requirements. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}
- **Use ECC and leave capacity margin** for blocks that become unusable. Merely replacing a bad block does not recover data that has already become uncorrectable. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

For your sequential-recording design, I would build this around a small block allocator rather than assume an SSD-style storage layer is necessary. **The simplification is in how you allocate and track blocks—not in assuming good blocks remain good forever.**

USER: does newly developed bad a block typically fail on the WRITE operation? Can they fail on READ and lose the data that was previously successfully written t to them?

ASSISTANT: **Yes—previously successfully written data can later become unreadable.** Program/erase failures are the standard trigger for retiring worn-out blocks, but **“the write succeeded, therefore the data is safe until I erase it” is not a valid NAND assumption.** Kioxia explicitly distinguishes program/erase failures from retention and read-disturb failures. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

### Three different failure paths

| Failure mode | What happens | When you discover it |
|---|---|---|
| **Program/erase failure** | The chip cannot successfully program a page or erase a block and reports failure. Firmware normally retires the block. | Immediately after the program/erase operation, by checking status. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"} |
| **Data-retention failure** | Stored charge changes over time, eventually changing the apparent bit values. This can happen without any intervening writes or reads. Retention depends on wear and temperature. | On a subsequent read. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"} |
| **Read disturb** | Repeated reads gradually disturb cells, generally in **other pages of the same block**. Enough accumulated errors can make previously written data unrecoverable. | On a subsequent read of the affected data. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"} |

So, distinguish **“failed during a read”** from **“damaged by a read.”** With retention loss, reading merely reveals corruption that has already occurred. With read disturb, repeated reading contributes to the corruption. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

### An unreadable page does not necessarily mean a permanently dead block

A block affected by retention loss or read disturb may become usable again after erasing and reprogramming it. **That does not recover its old contents.** Conversely, a correctable bit error need not cause any data loss or justify retiring the block. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

ECC is what separates ordinary raw bit errors from an unsuccessful read: within its correction capability, the original data is recovered; beyond that capability, recovery is not guaranteed, and another copy or additional redundancy may be needed. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

### Even a write-time failure can affect older data

A failure is not necessarily confined to the page currently being written. Swissbit documents some **3D NAND** failure modes in which an internal conductor fails during programming and makes the **entire block—including previously written pages—unreadable**. This is technology- and failure-mode-dependent, not a claim that every NAND program failure destroys the block’s contents. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}

### Implication for your recorder

**Checking every write and retrying elsewhere is necessary, but does not cover later read failures.** Read-back verification catches problems present at verification time; it cannot rule out subsequent retention loss. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="8"}

My design recommendation would be ECC plus independently recoverable recording chunks, so an unreadable chunk does not prevent decoding the rest. Protection against an entire block becoming unreadable requires additional redundancy—such as a copy in another block or parity across blocks—not merely a supply of spare blocks. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="9"}

## 2026-09-12T23:22:45.861Z · 9b0b6948-fb7f-4819-94b4-c0714c47a038

USER: so how do eg nand base hard drives deal with these read failures withoutu loosing saved data

ASSISTANT: **SSDs store extra recovery information when they write the data, then use it to repair errors before returning the data to your computer.** Some also store redundancy across different NAND pages, allowing recovery even when one page cannot be read at all. That is fundamentally different from merely replacing bad blocks with spare ones. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

There are four relevant mechanisms.

### 1. ECC repairs corrupted bits

The SSD stores each chunk of data together with **error-correcting code information**. On reading, the controller uses that extra information to reconstruct the original data even when some stored bits have changed. Within the code’s correction capability, this restores the **exact original bytes**, not an approximation. Modern SSD controllers use powerful codes such as LDPC. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

The important distinction is:

**A NAND read containing incorrect bits can still produce a completely successful, correct SSD read.**

However, local ECC cannot reconstruct an entire missing page if both its data and its ECC information are inaccessible. That requires redundancy stored elsewhere.

### 2. Read retries extract more information from marginal cells

An initially unsuccessful read does not necessarily mean the information is physically gone. The controller can use different sensing-voltage thresholds and more intensive decoding to recover marginal data. Silicon Motion, for example, describes an initial LDPC decoding stage followed by a stronger stage using voltage-threshold tracking. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

This is not just issuing the identical read repeatedly: it changes how the cells are measured and how those measurements are decoded.

### 3. Refresh moves data before it becomes unrecoverable

Controllers can monitor reads and scan stored data, looking for data that is becoming difficult to read. **While ECC can still recover it**, they copy the corrected data into healthy storage. ATP documents both read-count-triggered refresh and background scanning; Silicon Motion documents scanning and refreshing or retiring blocks according to operating conditions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

This is how gradual degradation can be handled without losing anything:

**Read marginal data → correct it → write a fresh copy → use the new location.**

The critical part is recovering the original contents *before* abandoning the old location. A blank replacement block, by itself, contains none of those contents.

### 4. Internal parity can reconstruct completely unreadable data

Some SSD designs add **RAID-like protection inside the drive**, beyond the ECC protecting individual chunks. Micron calls its internal XOR-based protection **RAIN: Redundant Array of Independent NAND**. Silicon Motion explicitly documents page parity as a recovery stage for data that its earlier correction stages cannot recover. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

A simplified illustration—not a specific SSD’s layout—is three data blocks plus a parity block:

```text
P = A XOR B XOR C
```

If block B becomes completely unreadable, but A, C, and P remain intact:

```text
B = P XOR A XOR C
```

**Nothing needs to be readable from B to reconstruct B.** The recovery information was stored elsewhere *before the failure*. This example can tolerate one known missing block; two missing blocks would exceed its protection.

Actual protection depends on how the SSD organizes its redundancy. A page-parity feature should not automatically be interpreted as protection against an entire chip failing.

### What happens when all that fails?

**The SSD can still lose saved data.** These mechanisms reduce the risk; they do not eliminate it. Manufacturers consequently specify an *uncorrectable bit error rate*, rather than promising error-free storage. Kioxia, for example, publishes such a rating for its enterprise drives despite their extensive protection features. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

For your raw-NAND recorder, the key distinction is **ECC for damaged bits versus separately stored redundancy for an unreadable page or block**. Following the chip’s ECC requirements addresses the first problem; it does not, by itself, provide the cross-page recovery layer found in more sophisticated SSD controllers. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

USER: how frequent are in the field read and write failures over the lifetime??

ASSISTANT: **Corrected read errors are routine. Failures that require retrying or retiring a block are much less frequent, but can affect a substantial fraction of drives over several years.** The important distinction is between a NAND-level error that the controller handles and a read that ultimately cannot return the saved data. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

## Actual multi-year field measurements

A Google/University of Toronto study published in **2016** measured custom data-center SSDs. For its eight SLC/MLC models, these were the fractions experiencing **at least one event during their first four years**:

| Event | Fraction of drives affected |
|---|---:|
| Read corruption successfully corrected by ECC | **96.1–99.9%** |
| Write failure subsequently recovered by retrying | **6.1–56.1%** |
| Erase failure | **4.8–68.4%** |
| Final read failure—not recovered by retries | **19.1–62.7%** |
| Final write failure—not recovered by retries | **0.57–2.39%** |

The ranges are across drive models. These are **percentages of drives experiencing an event, not percentages of operations failing or data being lost**. The figures come from Table 2, excluding the newer eMLC cohorts with shorter observation periods. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

**One correction to my earlier framing:** the authors inferred that most newly bad blocks in that fleet were discovered during **reads**, not writes or erases. So assuming that block failures will mostly announce themselves while writing is not supported by that field study. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

## Are those high read-failure numbers representative of current devices?

**They should not be treated as a current consumer-SSD failure probability—or a probability for one bare NAND chip.** They describe particular older controllers, NAND generations, capacities, and data-center workloads.

More recent evidence does confirm that read-related problems remain important. A **2026 Samsung/Tencent study**, using telemetry from more than **350,000 Samsung PM9A3 SSDs**, found that among drives whose bad-block count increased, **73% also showed increased read-retry counts**, while **30% showed increased program-failure counts**. Those groups overlap. This does **not** mean that 73% of all drives lost data: retries can recover the data, and the denominator is specifically drives developing additional bad blocks. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

Also, **whole-drive replacement rates are a different statistic**. A 2020 University of Toronto/NetApp field study reported an average annual replacement rate of **0.22%**, with substantial model variation. A drive can experience corrected errors or retire blocks without needing replacement; conversely, replacement can result from problems other than NAND data corruption. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

## What can we infer for your raw-NAND recorder?

**Not a defensible numerical probability yet.** The field studies establish that these failure modes matter, but they do not provide a workload-matched lifetime rate for a small recorder using a particular raw NAND chip.

For raw NAND, the manufacturer’s useful specifications include the required ECC strength, program/erase endurance, and minimum number of valid blocks. For example, Micron explicitly defines its minimum-valid-block specification as applying **at the end of the specified program/erase endurance**, and requires checking program/erase status and retiring failed blocks. These are operating requirements and limits—not a measured forecast of how frequently your particular recorder will encounter failures. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

Those are workload calculations, **not failure-probability estimates**. They help evaluate the wear budget, but do not establish how likely previously written data is to become unreadable.
