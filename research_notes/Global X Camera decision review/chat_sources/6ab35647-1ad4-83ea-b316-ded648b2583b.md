## Best sensor to build around: TI OPT4001

The OPT4001 has optical filtering intended to closely follow the human eye’s **photopic response**, with strong infrared rejection. It provides an I²C output, automatic range selection, and conversion times from **600 µs to 800 ms**. However, it is **not inherently narrow-angle**: its specified angular response is **120° full width at half maximum in the SOT package**, or **96° in PicoStar**. External optics would be doing the narrowing. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

I would choose the **SOT-5X3 package** for a prototype: its light-sensitive surface faces upward, whereas the PicoStar version receives light from the PCB side and normally requires a board cutout. The datasheet shows a sensing area approximately **0.30 × 0.38 mm**, which is small enough to get a narrow view with compact optics. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

A lower-power alternative is the **TI OPT3001**. It also has photopic filtering and I²C output, but draws **1.8 µA typical operating current**, versus **30 µA** for the OPT4001. It would likewise need an external FOV limiter. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

## Getting below 10° can be physically small

**A blackened tube with an entrance aperture is the simplest approach.** As an illustrative geometric design, a **1 mm diameter aperture positioned 10 mm in front of a detector roughly 0.5 mm across** has a full direct-ray acceptance limit of approximately:

\[
\theta_{\mathrm{full}}\approx
2\arctan\!\left(\frac{D+s}{2L}\right)
=
2\arctan\!\left(\frac{1+0.5}{20}\right)
\approx 8.6^\circ
\]

Here \(D\) is the entrance-aperture diameter, \(s\) is the detector’s maximum span, and \(L\) is their separation. This is a **geometric estimate**, assuming an absorbing enclosure and ignoring package refraction and scattering—not a guaranteed angular-response specification. Sensitivity rolls off before that limit rather than remaining flat and then abruptly stopping.

For a prototype, I would test that arrangement first, with a matte-black interior and baffling to prevent reflected off-axis light from reaching the sensor. TI specifically cautions that housings and apertures alter both angular response and measurement performance. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

**A small lens is the other option**, particularly when collecting more light from the selected direction matters. With the detector at the focal plane, the first-order relationship is:

\[
\mathrm{FOV}\approx2\arctan\!\left(\frac{\text{detector dimension}}{2f}\right)
\]

For the OPT4001’s approximately **0.30 × 0.38 mm** sensing area, a **5 mm focal-length lens** gives a calculated distant-object FOV of roughly **3.4° × 4.4°**, or **5.5° diagonally**. That is an optical-design starting point; focus, lens aberrations, encapsulation, and stray-light rejection still need checking. **Focal length is not the lens diameter.** :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

## Beware of “narrow-angle” and “human-eye matched” marketing

One tempting leaded part is the **Vishay TEPT5600**, a 5 mm phototransistor. But its specified half-sensitivity angle is **±20°**, meaning **40° full width**, not 20° and certainly not below 10°. Although marketed as adapted to human-eye sensitivity, its spectral curve retains substantial near-infrared sensitivity; I would not choose it where faithful perceived-brightness measurement matters. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

Also, the spectral characteristic you want is presumably **CIE photopic \(V(\lambda)\) weighting**, not a flat response across the visible band. That weighting peaks around **555 nm** and falls toward blue/violet and deep red. A sensor merely being “visible sensitive” does not establish that match. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

**My practical starting point: OPT4001 in the SOT package, behind a roughly 10 mm-long blackened aperture assembly.** The complete assembly should be calibrated and its angular response measured; restricting the view means its reading no longer represents ordinary wide-angle ambient illuminance. Lens-equipped photopic detectors are commonly used instead for directional **luminance** measurements. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}
